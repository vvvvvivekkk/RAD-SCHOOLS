# Story Bank — AI for Grades 5–10 (Telangana private schools)

**Status:** COMPLETE — 31 entries. First full draft, 21 November 2026.

**What this is.** Classroom stories a trainer tells out loud: to open a lesson, to
make an abstract idea concrete, or to start an argument. Written for English-medium
Grades 5–10 (ages ~10–16), mostly CBSE, Siddipet and Hyderabad. Telugu is the home
language for most students, so stories are kept short, in plain English, with any
technical word defined the first time it appears.

**How to use an entry.** Tell the story (60–90 seconds). Ask the question. Do not
answer it. Let students disagree. The question is designed to have no single right
answer.

## Conventions used in every entry

- **Evidence label** — one of FACT / ESTIMATE / ASSUMPTION / OPINION.
- **Source type** — peer-reviewed / government or official release / court or
  tribunal record / journalism / company or project claim / NGO research / unverified.
- **Scope** — **India-specific** or **Global**.
- **Accessed** — every link was opened on **21 November 2026**. If a link later dies,
  the entry still carries enough detail to re-find the source.
- **Quotes** — at most one quote per source, under 15 words. Most entries carry none.
- **Flags** — `⚠ MYTH / EXAGGERATED` marks a story students have probably heard that
  is wrong or overstated. These are kept on purpose: a wrong story a student already
  believes is the best teaching material in this file.
- **Press-release warning** — where the only evidence is an announcement rather than a
  measured result, the entry says so.

## Safety rules applied

- No private individual who lost money, was wrongly arrested, or was targeted by a
  deepfake is named here, even where news reports name them. Public figures acting in
  public (an actor whose case triggered a national advisory, an activist who filed his
  own court petition, a minister) are named.
- Fraud, deepfakes and surveillance are told to inform, not to frighten. Nothing
  graphic. Nothing about violence or self-harm.
- Student achievements ARE named, because that is the point of Category 4 — but only
  where a government document, a school's own page, or a news report names them.

## 1. The Chilli Farmers of Khammam

- **Grade band:** 5-6
- **Topic tag:** Indian AI
- **Scope:** India-specific (Telangana)

**Story.** Khammam district in Telangana grows chillies. A few years ago, more than
seven thousand chilli farmers there joined a project called Saagu Baagu. They got
soil tests, advice on their phones, and a quality check on their harvest — all built
on software that studied data from thousands of fields. Here is what the project
reported afterwards: yield per acre went up by about 21%. Pesticide use went *down* by
about 9%. Fertiliser use went down by about 5%. And because the chillies were better
quality, farmers got about 8% better prices. Put together, the project says a farmer
earned around ₹66,000 more per acre in one crop cycle. The Telangana government liked
it enough to expand it to five crops across ten districts.

**Ask the class.** The project says the farmers earned more. But the advice came from
a company's software, not from the farmer's own experience. If the software is wrong
one year and a farmer loses the crop, whose fault is it — the farmer who followed the
advice, the company that wrote the software, or the government that recommended it?

- **Source:** World Economic Forum, "AI for agriculture: How Indian farmers are
  harvesting innovation", 11 January 2024 (page last updated 2 April 2026) —
  https://www.weforum.org/stories/2024/01/ai-for-agriculture-in-india/ — **accessed 21
  November 2026**
- **Evidence label:** FACT that the project ran and reported these results; the
  numbers themselves are **ESTIMATE** — they are the project's own reported figures.
- **Source type:** NGO/forum report. Partners named are the Government of Telangana,
  Digital Green, the Gates Foundation and the WEF's AI4AI initiative. **Not an
  independent evaluation.** Treat the 21% and the ₹66,000 as the project's claim.

## 2. Ask the Government in Your Own Words

- **Grade band:** 7-8
- **Topic tag:** how chatbots work
- **Scope:** India-specific

**Story.** PM-KISAN is a central government scheme that pays money straight into
farmers' bank accounts. Farmers kept asking the same questions: has my money come? why
was my application rejected? So in 2023 the Ministry of Agriculture put an AI chatbot
on it, called Kisan e-Mitra. A **chatbot** is a program you type or talk to, that
answers in sentences instead of making you click through menus. By January 2024 the
government said more than 21 lakh farmers had used it within two months. It worked in
five languages: Hindi, Tamil, Odia, Bangla and English. Notice which language is
missing from that list.

**Ask the class.** Telugu was not in the first five languages. If you were the officer
choosing which five languages to build first, and you only had money for five, how
would you choose — by number of speakers, by number of farmers in the scheme, by which
states complain loudest, or some other way? Is any of those fair?

- **Source:** Press Information Bureau, Ministry of Agriculture & Farmers Welfare,
  release PRID 1997066, 17 January 2024 —
  https://pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1997066 — **accessed 21 November 2026**
- **Evidence label:** FACT (the chatbot exists and the government stated these
  figures). The usage figure is the government's own count, so treat the *impact* as
  ESTIMATE.
- **Source type:** Government release. ⚠ **Press-release caution:** the release reports
  access numbers, not whether farmers' problems were actually solved.

## 3. Thirty-Six Languages, One Button

- **Grade band:** 5-6
- **Topic tag:** speech and language
- **Scope:** India-specific

**Story.** India has 22 official languages and hundreds more that people actually
speak. Most of the internet is in English. So the Government of India built something
called Bhashini — a free translation and speech service that any website or app can
plug into. As of March 2026 the government said Bhashini handles 36 written languages
and 23 spoken ones, including some tribal languages, and runs on more than 500
government websites. It does more than 15 million **inferences** a day — an inference
is one job, like one sentence translated or one spoken line turned into text. Total so
far: more than 6 billion.

**Ask the class.** If a government form can now be auto-translated into Telugu
instantly, do we still need government officers who speak Telugu? What can a human
officer do that the translation cannot?

- **Source:** Press Information Bureau, Ministry of Electronics & IT, release PRID
  2239132, 12 March 2026 — https://pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2239132 —
  **accessed 21 November 2026**
- **Evidence label:** FACT (official figures, officially stated).
- **Source type:** Government release. Note: this particular release does **not** name
  Telugu specifically. Telugu support exists on the Bhashini platform, but if you want
  to claim it in class, demonstrate it live rather than citing this release.

## 4. Seven Thousand Hours of Indian Voices

- **Grade band:** 9-10
- **Topic tag:** data and labels
- **Scope:** India-specific

**Story.** If you want a computer to understand Telugu speech, you first need
recordings of Telugu being spoken — thousands of hours of it, with someone writing
down exactly what was said. That written-down version is called a **transcript**, and
the pair of recording-plus-transcript is the **labelled data** the computer learns
from. A team at IIT Madras called AI4Bharat, with Sarvam AI, went and collected it.
They ended up with 7,348 hours of speech, in 22 Indian languages, from 16,237 people
across 145 districts. They did not just ask people to read sentences. They asked them
to tell stories, give instructions to an imaginary assistant, role-play a customer
care call, and argue a point. Every single recording was then checked against 23
different kinds of error.

**Ask the class.** They paid and organised ordinary people in 145 districts to speak
into a phone. Those people's voices now sit inside AI products that companies sell.
How much of the value should go back to the person who spoke? Anything? Nothing? And
who decides?

- **Source:** AI4Bharat (IIT Madras) and Sarvam AI, "INDICVOICES: Towards building an
  Inclusive Multilingual Speech Dataset for Indian Languages", *Findings of the
  Association for Computational Linguistics: ACL 2024*, pp. 10740–10782 —
  https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.639.pdf — **accessed 21 November 2026**
- **Evidence label:** FACT.
- **Source type:** Peer-reviewed (ACL Findings 2024). Institutions: AI4Bharat, IIT
  Madras, Sarvam AI.

## 5. The X-ray That Got a Second Opinion

- **Grade band:** 9-10
- **Topic tag:** evaluation
- **Scope:** India-specific

**Story.** Tuberculosis — TB — is a lung disease India is trying to wipe out. One way
to find it is a chest X-ray, read by a radiologist. In Nagpur, Maharashtra, a project
ran from 2018 to 2020 in crowded low-income neighbourhoods. Local informal health
workers sent 10,481 people who might have TB for a check. 8,731 of them got a chest
X-ray. Each X-ray was read twice: once by a human radiologist, and once by software
called qXR. 2,303 X-rays were flagged as suspicious by the doctor, or the software, or
both. In the end 213 people were confirmed to have TB. Here is the interesting bit: 29
of those cases were flagged *only* by the software and not by the radiologist. The
researchers calculated that the software added about a 15.8% increase in cases found.

**Ask the class.** The software found 29 cases the doctor missed. It also flagged
people who turned out not to have TB, and each of those people had to go for more
tests and worry for a week. If you were running the programme, how many false alarms
would you accept to catch one extra real case — 5? 50? 500? Is there a number where it
becomes wrong?

- **Source:** Authors from Qure.ai, PATH, John Snow Inc., Nagpur Municipal Corporation, WHO
  India and the Gates Foundation, "Implementing a chest X-ray artificial
  intelligence tool to enhance tuberculosis screening in India: Lessons learned",
  *PLOS Digital Health*, 2(12): e0000404, 7 December 2023 —
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10703224 — **accessed 21 November 2026**
- **Evidence label:** FACT.
- **Source type:** Peer-reviewed. Note the author list includes staff of Qure.ai, the
  company that makes qXR — a conflict of interest worth naming to a Grade 10 class.

## 6. Two Hospitals, Two Different Scores

- **Grade band:** 9-10
- **Topic tag:** training vs testing
- **Scope:** India-specific

**Story.** Diabetes can quietly damage the back of the eye. Caught early, it is
treatable; caught late, people go blind. India does not have enough eye specialists to
check everyone, so researchers tested software that reads photographs of the retina —
the light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye. They tested it at two Indian
hospitals: Aravind Eye Hospital in Madurai and Sankara Nethralaya in Chennai. 3,049
patients. Same software, same settings. At Aravind it caught 88.9% of the cases it
should have caught. At Sankara, 92.1%. And at Sankara the software actually beat the
retina specialist's own detection rate. The *same program* performed differently in two
cities in the same country.

**Ask the class.** The software scored differently at two hospitals in India. Now
imagine it being brought to a government hospital in Siddipet, with older cameras and
different lighting. Should the hospital be allowed to use it before it has been tested
*there*? Who should pay for that test?

- **Source:** Gulshan V, Rajan RP, Widner K, et al., "Performance of a Deep-Learning
  Algorithm vs Manual Grading for Detecting Diabetic Retinopathy in India", *JAMA
  Ophthalmology*, 137(9):987–993, published online 13 June 2019 —
  https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamaophthalmology/fullarticle/2734990 — **accessed
  21 November 2026**
- **Evidence label:** FACT.
- **Source type:** Peer-reviewed. Authors from Google Research, Verily, Aravind Eye
  Hospital and Sankara Nethralaya — again, company co-authorship worth flagging.

## 7. The Dashcam Traffic Cop

- **Grade band:** 7-8
- **Topic tag:** computer vision
- **Scope:** India-specific (Telangana)

**Story.** Telangana Police have a problem every city has: too many traffic
violations, not enough officers standing at junctions. So researchers at IIIT
Hyderabad built a system called VIOLA. A cheap dashcam — about ₹8,000 to ₹10,000 — goes
into a police patrol car. As the car drives around Hyderabad, the camera records.
Software watches the footage and spots riders without helmets, three people on one
bike, and vehicles going the wrong way. It reads the number plate automatically, packs
the evidence together, and pushes it into the police e-challan system. An officer still
checks every single one before a fine is issued. The team says about 96% of what the
software submits is accurate enough for the officer to approve. Only four dashcams were
installed — and they became one of the biggest sources of challans in the state.

**Ask the class.** Four cameras, thousands of fines. The officer only *checks* the
machine's work instead of spotting violations himself. Is the officer still the one
deciding? If the software is right 96 times out of 100, what happens to the 4 people?

- **Source:** Telangana Today, "AI traffic cop Viola helps Telangana Police detect
  violations using dashcams", 21 July 2026 —
  https://telanganatoday.com/ai-traffic-cop-viola-helps-telangana-police-detect-violations-using-dashcams
  — **accessed 21 November 2026**
- **Evidence label:** FACT that the system is deployed. The 96% accuracy figure is an
  **ESTIMATE** stated by the builders, not independently audited.
- **Source type:** Journalism, reporting a claim by IIIT Hyderabad's Applied AI
  Research Centre (INAI) and Telangana Police.

## 8. The River Nobody Measured

- **Grade band:** 9-10
- **Topic tag:** AI in daily life
- **Scope:** Global, with an India hook

**Story.** To warn a village that a flood is coming, you normally need a gauge in the
river upstream — a measuring station. Huge numbers of rivers in the world have no
gauge at all, so there is nothing to warn from. In 2024 a team published a method in
the journal *Nature* that predicts extreme floods in **ungauged** basins — rivers with
no measuring station — by learning patterns from the rivers that *do* have gauges.
Google says it uses this to send flood alerts in India, working with the Central Water
Commission. Meanwhile in Hyderabad the problem is different: the city floods from
rainfall in its own streets. GHMC's disaster force has sent heavy-rain SMS warnings to
around 25 lakh people in Hyderabad, Rangareddy and Medchal in a single day. And at IIT
Hyderabad a group is building an Urban Flood Information System that collects flood
reports from citizens through an app called SnapFlood and from social media.

**Ask the class.** A warning only helps if people act on it. 25 lakh people got an SMS
about heavy rain. Most of them probably ignored it. How would you design a warning
that people believe — and what is the cost of warnings that turn out to be wrong?

- **Sources:**
  - Nearing G. et al., "Global prediction of extreme floods in ungauged
    watersheds", *Nature*, 627:559–563, 2024 —
    https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07145-1.pdf — **accessed 21 November
    2026** — peer-reviewed
  - Google Research blog, "Using AI to expand global access to reliable flood
    forecasts" —
    https://research.google/blog/using-ai-to-expand-global-access-to-reliable-flood-forecasts/
    — **accessed 21 November 2026** — **company claim** for the India/CWC deployment
  - Telangana Today, "Hyderabad rains: 25 lakh people receive GHMC's SMS warning on
    heavy rains", 21 July 2023 —
    https://telanganatoday.com/hyderabad-rains-25-lakh-people-receive-ghmcs-sms-warning-on-heavy-rains
    — **accessed 21 November 2026** — journalism

    October 2023 —
    https://telanganatoday.com/iith-embarks-on-pioneering-urban-flood-mitigation-project-to-bolster-hyderabads-resilience
    — **accessed 21 November 2026** — journalism

## 9. One Gigawatt in Hyderabad

- **Grade band:** 9-10
- **Topic tag:** AI and the environment
- **Scope:** India-specific (Telangana)

**Story.** Every AI answer you get is computed in a building full of machines called a
**data centre**. Hyderabad already has more than 30 of them. In 2026 it was reported
that the city will host a one-gigawatt data centre campus — one of India's largest. A
gigawatt is a very large amount of electricity; for comparison, the International
Energy Agency expects the world's data centres to use roughly 945 terawatt-hours of
electricity by 2030, about 3% of all electricity on Earth. Then there is water, used
to carry heat away. Using IEA estimates, a 100-megawatt data centre may use around 2
million litres of water a day. Scale that to a gigawatt and you get roughly 20 million
litres a day — about 7.3 billion litres a year. Hyderabad already has water stress.
Telangana's stated target is 5 gigawatts of data centre capacity by 2029.

**Ask the class.** The data centre brings thousands of jobs to Hyderabad. It also
drinks water in a city that runs short every summer. If you were the Chief Minister,
what would you demand from the company before you said yes — and would any condition
be enough?

- **Source:** Telangana Today, "Hyderabad to host 1 GW data centre: Promises jobs, but
  environmental concerns loom", 6 September 2026 —
  https://telanganatoday.com/hyderabad-to-host-1-gw-data-centre-promises-jobs-but-environmental-concerns-loom
  — **accessed 21 November 2026**
- **Evidence label:** FACT that the project and the 5 GW target were announced.
  **ESTIMATE** for every water and power figure — they are IEA projections applied by
  the reporter to a facility not yet built, not measured consumption.
- **Source type:** Journalism, quoting IEA projections (official body) and a named
  public policy expert, Dr. Donthi Narasimha Reddy.
- ⚠ **Press-release caution:** the data centre is announced, not operating. Do not tell
  students it already uses 20 million litres a day.

## 10. The Algorithm That Said She Owned a Car

- **Grade band:** 9-10
- **Topic tag:** confidence vs correctness
- **Scope:** India-specific (Telangana)

**Story.** Telangana has a system called Samagra Vedika. It reads across as many as 30
government databases and tries to work out which records belong to the same person —
your ration card, your property tax, your vehicle registration. That matching job is
called **entity resolution**, and it uses machine learning. The government used it to
check who really deserved a food security card. Between 2014 and 2019 Telangana
cancelled more than 18.6 lakh existing food security cards and rejected 1,42,086 new
applications. The state said these were fraudulent claims. Journalists investigating in
2024 found that several thousand of those exclusions were simply wrong — a car
belonging to someone with a similar name had been matched to the wrong family; a dead
man was matched to a living car owner. After the Supreme Court ordered a
re-verification of about 19 lakh deleted cards, 15,471 of the 2,05,734 applications
processed by July 2022 were approved — meaning at least 7.5% had been wrongly
rejected. Officials often believed the system over the person standing in front of
them. When people asked to see the source code, the state said the company owned it.

**Ask the class.** The government says the system has "high precision". Suppose it is
right 95 times in 100. In a state of 3.5 crore people, who should carry the burden of
proving the other 5 wrong — the family, or the government? And should a citizen have
the right to see the code that cut off their food?

- **Source:** Al Jazeera, "How an algorithm denied food to thousands of poor in India's
  Telangana", by Tapasya, Kumar Sambhav and Divij Joshi, 24 January 2024 —
  https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2024/1/24/how-an-algorithm-denied-food-to-thousands-of-poor-in-indias-telangana
  — **accessed 21 November 2026**
- **Supporting:** Amnesty International, "Entity resolution in India's welfare
  digitalization", April 2024 —
  https://amnesty.org/en/latest/research/2024/04/entity-resolution-in-indias-welfare-digitalization
  — **accessed 21 November 2026** — NGO research
- **Evidence label:** FACT for the cancellation counts and the re-verification figures
  (court-ordered process, officially recorded). **ESTIMATE** for "several thousand
  wrongful exclusions" — that is the journalists' finding. The government's "high
  precision" and "under 5% mis-tagging" claims are **company/government claims**, not
  independently verified.
- **Source type:** Investigative journalism (Pulitzer Center supported), plus NGO
  research. The vendor named is Posidex Technologies Pvt Ltd.
- **No individuals named here.** Al Jazeera names affected families; this entry
  deliberately does not.

## 11. The Video Call From a Friend Who Wasn't

- **Grade band:** 7-8
- **Topic tag:** deepfakes and misinformation
- **Scope:** India-specific

**Story.** In July 2023 a retired official in Kozhikode, Kerala, got a WhatsApp video
call. On the screen was a former colleague's face, speaking to him. The colleague said
he needed money urgently for a relative's hospital bill. The man sent ₹40,000. Then
came a second request for more — and that is when he got suspicious and called the real
colleague, who knew nothing about it. The face on the video call had been generated
from photographs. This was widely reported as India's first deepfake video-call fraud.
Kerala's cyber police traced the money and recovered it; the investigation spread to
another state. A **deepfake** is a fake picture, video or voice made by software so it
looks and sounds like a real person.

**Ask the class.** He did the right thing eventually — he rang the real person. But
that only worked because he had the real person's number. Invent a rule your own family
could use, in one sentence, that would stop this. Then try to break your own rule.

- **Source:** Onmanorama, "Coal India's retd official loses Rs 40K after he gets
  deep-fake video call from ex-colleague asking for money", 17 July 2023 —
  https://www.onmanorama.com/news/kerala/2023/07/17/ai-based-deep-fake-man-conned-kozhikode-account-owner.html
  — **accessed 21 November 2026**
- **Supporting:** Deccan Herald, "Fraud using AI-based deepfaking reported in Kerala" —
  https://www.deccanherald.com/india/fraud-using-ai-based-deepfaking-reported-in-kerala-1237663.html
  — **accessed 21 November 2026**
- **Evidence label:** FACT (police case, recovery confirmed in reporting). The claim
  that it was India's *first* such case is **ESTIMATE / contested** — say "one of the
  first reported", not "the first".
- **Source type:** Journalism.
- **The victim is not named in this entry**, although news reports identify him.

## 12. Thirty-Six Hours to Take It Down

- **Grade band:** 7-8
- **Topic tag:** deepfakes and misinformation
- **Scope:** India-specific

**Story.** In November 2023 a video spread across Indian social media showing the
actor Rashmika Mandanna. It was not her. Someone had taken a video of a different
person and pasted her face onto it using deepfake software. She said publicly that it
frightened her. The reaction was fast and official: India's Ministry of Electronics and
Information Technology sent an advisory to social media companies reminding them that
under India's IT Rules they must remove such content within 36 hours of being
reported, and that failing to do so can cost them their legal protection. One fake
video of one famous person changed how an entire country's platforms were expected to
behave.

**Ask the class.** The rule is "take it down within 36 hours of a report". By then the
video has usually been copied a thousand times. Is 36 hours a real protection, or just
a number that sounds like action? What would you replace it with — and what would your
rule cost?

- **Source:** Business Standard, "MeitY issues advisory to social media cos after
  Rashmika's controversy", 7 November 2023 —
  https://www.business-standard.com/india-news/meity-issues-advisory-to-social-media-cos-after-rashmika-s-controversy-123110700598_1.html

## 13. Leaders Who Spoke After They Died

## 14. The Phone Call That Pretends to Be the Police

- **Grade band:** 9-10
- **Topic tag:** AI in daily life
- **Scope:** India-specific

**Story.** A "digital arrest" scam works like this: someone calls claiming to be from
the police or a courier company, says a parcel in your name contains something illegal,
keeps you on a video call for hours so you cannot consult anyone, and frightens you

catching, or on teaching every student in Telangana to recognise the script?

## 16. 0.8% and 34.7%

- **Grade band:** 7-8
- **Topic tag:** bias and fairness
- **Scope:** Global

**Story.** In 2018 two researchers, Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru, tested three
commercial face-analysis products. The products had one job: look at a photo of a face
and say whether it is a man or a woman. The researchers noticed something first: the
standard test sets everyone used were overwhelmingly made of lighter-skinned faces —
79.6% in one, 86.2% in another. So they built their own test set, balanced for skin
tone and gender, and ran the three products on it. On lighter-skinned men, the worst
error rate was 0.8% — basically never wrong. On darker-skinned women, the error rate
went up to 34.7% — wrong about one time in three. Same software. Same task.

**Ask the class.** The companies had tested their products and found them accurate. The
researchers tested them and found them broken. Nobody lied. So what went wrong — and
who should have caught it?

- **Source:** Buolamwini J, Gebru T, "Gender Shades: Intersectional Accuracy
  Disparities in Commercial Gender Classification", *Proceedings of the 1st Conference
  on Fairness, Accountability and Transparency*, PMLR 81:77–91, 2018 —
  https://proceedings.mlr.press/v81/buolamwini18a.html — **accessed 21 November 2026**
- **Evidence label:** FACT.
- **Source type:** Peer-reviewed.

## 17. The Hiring Robot That Learned Who Got Hired Before

- **Grade band:** 9-10
- **Topic tag:** data and labels
- **Scope:** Global

**Story.** Around 2014 Amazon started building software to sort job applications
automatically — give it a stack of CVs, get back the best ones. To teach it what "good"
looked like, engineers fed it the CVs the company had received over the previous ten
years and which of those people had been hired. Most of them were men, because most
people hired in that industry over those ten years were men. The software learned the
pattern perfectly, and that was the problem: it began marking down CVs that contained
the word "women's", as in "women's chess club captain". Amazon's engineers tried to
patch it. They could not be sure they had found every version of the same bias, and the
project was scrapped by 2017. Reuters reported it in 2018.

**Ask the class.** The software was not told to prefer men. It was only told "copy what
we did before". If a system that copies the past is unfair whenever the past was
unfair, can any system that learns from history ever be fair? What would you feed it
instead?

- **Source:** Jeffrey Dastin, Reuters, "Amazon scraps secret AI recruiting tool that
  showed bias against women", 10 October 2018. Reuters' own page has moved; a stable
  mirror of the full text:
  https://ecampus.paris-saclay.fr/pluginfile.php/3524211/mod_folder/content/0/Algos-%20Amazon%20scraps%20secret%20AI%20recruiting%20tool%20that%20showed%20bias%20against%20women%20-%20Reuters.pdf
  — **accessed 21 November 2026**. Independent same-day report: CNBC —
  https://www.cnbc.com/2018/10/10/amazon-scraps-a-secret-ai-recruiting-tool-that-showed-bias-against-women.html
  — **accessed 21 November 2026**
- **Evidence label:** FACT that Reuters reported this, based on named-but-anonymous
  insiders. **ESTIMATE** for the mechanism details — Amazon never published the system

- **Source type:** Journalism (investigative, sourced to unnamed insiders).

## 18. The Health Score That Measured Money

- **Grade band:** 9-10
- **Topic tag:** bias and fairness
- **Scope:** Global

**Story.** American hospitals use software to decide which patients are sick enough to
need extra care. In 2019 researchers published a study in the journal *Science* taking
apart one widely used system. The software did not try to predict how sick you were. It
predicted how much your care would **cost** — because cost data was easy to get, and
you would think sicker people cost more. But in the United States, less money gets
spent on Black patients for the same illness, because of unequal access to care. So
when the software saw a low cost, it concluded "not very sick". The result: at the same
risk score, Black patients were measurably sicker than White patients. The researchers
worked out that fixing this one substitution would raise the share of Black patients
getting extra help from 17.7% to 46.5%.

**Ask the class.** The engineers used "cost" because they could not measure "how sick".
Name something in your own school that gets measured by a stand-in like that — marks
standing in for understanding, attendance standing in for effort. Who gets hurt by the
stand-in?

- **Source:** Obermeyer Z, Powers B, Vogeli C, Mullainathan S, "Dissecting racial bias
  in an algorithm used to manage the health of populations", *Science*,
  366(6464):447–453, 25 October 2019 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31649194/ —
  **accessed 21 November 2026**
- **Evidence label:** FACT.
- **Source type:** Peer-reviewed.
- **Delivery note:** this story is about race in the United States. For an Indian
  classroom, land the transferable idea — a **proxy**, a stand-in measurement — rather
  than the American racial politics.

## 19. Six Cases That Did Not Exist

- **Grade band:** 9-10
- **Topic tag:** hallucinations and fake citations
- **Scope:** Global

**Story.** In 2023 two lawyers in New York filed a legal argument in a real court case
against an airline. To support it they cited past judgments — the normal thing lawyers
do. The other side could not find the judgments. Neither could the judge. Six of them
did not exist. One lawyer had asked ChatGPT to find supporting cases, and the chatbot
had produced cases with real-sounding names, real-sounding court numbers, and quotes
that read like a judge wrote them. He had even asked the chatbot whether the cases were
real; it said yes. The judge, P. Kevin Castel, fined the lawyers and their firm $5,000
and wrote an opinion about it in June 2023. A **hallucination** is when an AI system
produces something fluent and confident that is simply made up.

**Ask the class.** He asked the chatbot "are these real?" and it said yes. That was the
mistake. So: what question *could* he have asked the chatbot that would have caught the
problem? Or is the honest answer that no question to the chatbot would have worked?

- **Source:** *Mata v. Avianca, Inc.*, No. 1:22-cv-01461 (S.D.N.Y.), Opinion and Order
  on Sanctions, Document 54, 22 June 2023 —

  — **accessed 21 November 2026**. Full PDF of the order:
  https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.575368/gov.uscourts.nysd.575368.54.0_1.pdf
  — **accessed 21 November 2026**
- **Evidence label:** FACT.
- **Source type:** Court record (primary).

## 20. The Chatbot That Invented a Refund

- **Grade band:** 7-8
- **Topic tag:** confidence vs correctness
- **Scope:** Global

**Story.** A man in British Columbia, Canada, needed to fly for a family funeral. He
asked Air Canada's website chatbot about discounted bereavement fares, and the chatbot
told him he could buy a full-price ticket now and apply for the discount within 90
days. That was not the airline's actual policy. When he applied, Air Canada refused. He
took it to a small-claims tribunal. Air Canada's argument was remarkable: the chatbot,
it said, was a separate entity responsible for its own actions. The tribunal rejected
that flatly — the chatbot is part of Air Canada's website, so Air Canada is responsible
for what it says, exactly as it would be for a printed page. On 14 February 2024 the
airline was ordered to pay $812.02.

**Ask the class.** If a company's chatbot tells you something wrong, who should pay —
the company, the company that built the chatbot, or you for believing it? Now answer
the same question about a chatbot your school puts on its website for parents.

- **Source:** *Moffatt v. Air Canada*, 2024 BCCRT 149, British Columbia Civil
  Resolution Tribunal, 14 February 2024 —
  https://decisions.civilresolutionbc.ca/crt/crtd/en/525448/1/document.do — **accessed
  21 November 2026**
- **Evidence label:** FACT.
- **Source type:** Tribunal record (primary).

## 21. The Cheating Detector That Accused Honest Students

- **Grade band:** 9-10
- **Topic tag:** evaluation
- **Scope:** Global — but this one hits Indian students directly

**Story.** When chatbots arrived, companies started selling "AI detectors" to schools:
paste in an essay, and the tool tells you whether a machine wrote it. In 2023
researchers at Stanford tested seven of these detectors. First they ran them on 91
essays written by real students taking TOEFL — an English test taken by people whose
first language is not English. The detectors wrongly called those human essays
"AI-generated" 61.3% of the time, on average. All seven detectors agreed on the wrong
answer for 19.8% of them, and at least one detector flagged 97.8% of them. Then the
researchers ran the same tools on essays by American eighth-graders writing in their
first language. The tools got those almost perfectly right. The detectors were not
detecting AI. They were detecting simpler vocabulary — which is what writing in your
second language looks like.

**Ask the class.** Nearly every student in this room writes English as a second or
third language. If your school bought one of these tools tomorrow, who in this room
would get accused first? And what evidence would you want your school to require before
it punished anyone?

- **Source:** Liang W, Yuksekgonul M, Mao Y, Wu E, Zou J, "GPT detectors are biased
  against non-native English writers", *Patterns*, 4(7):100779, 10 July 2023 —
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10382961/ — **accessed 21 November 2026**
- **Evidence label:** FACT.
- **Source type:** Peer-reviewed.
- **Programme note:** this entry should also drive policy. The programme should not
  recommend AI-detector tools to partner schools, and this paper is the reason to put in
  the proposal.

## 22. The Robots That "Invented Their Own Language" ⚠ MYTH / EXAGGERATED

- **Grade band:** 5-6
- **Topic tag:** what AI is and isn't
- **Scope:** Global

**Story.** You may have heard this one: in 2017 Facebook's researchers built two
chatbots, the bots started talking to each other in a secret language humans could not
understand, the scientists panicked and pulled the plug. It is a great story. It is
wrong. What actually happened: researchers were training two programs to haggle with
each other — to negotiate swapping items. Nobody had told them to keep using proper
English, so their messages drifted into repetitive, clipped nonsense that worked for
them but was useless for the real goal, which was negotiating *with people*. So the
researchers changed the setup to force readable English. The project was not shut down
in fear. There was no secret plot. The press reported it as if the machines had turned
on us, and that version is the one that spread.

**Ask the class.** Thousands of news sites published the scary version. Almost nobody
published the correction. If you wanted to find out which version was true — today,
with the tools you actually have — what exactly would you do first?

- **Sources:**

    August 2017 —

    — **accessed 21 November 2026**
  - The Register, "Facebook pulls plug on language-inventing chatbots? THE TRUTH", 1
    August 2017 —
    https://www.theregister.co.uk/2017/08/01/facebook_chatbots_did_not_invent_new_language/
    — **accessed 21 November 2026**

- **Evidence label:** the scary version is **false**. The corrected version is FACT.
- **Source type:** Journalism and fact-check.

## 25. The Handwritten Complaint Machine

# How a trainer uses this file

1. **One story per session, not three.** 60–90 seconds of telling, then the question,
   then ten minutes of students disagreeing. The story is the hook, not the lesson.
2. **Never answer the question.** If the room splits two ways and nobody wins, the entry
   worked.
3. **Tell students where it comes from.** "This is from a government press release" and
   "this is from a peer-reviewed paper" and "this is from a newspaper" mean different
   things, and Grade 9 can hold that difference. Saying it out loud every time is half the
   media-literacy syllabus, delivered free.
4. **Use the flags.** Where an entry says a figure is the project's own claim, say so.
   Where it says a story is a myth, let students argue for the myth first.
5. **Match the grade band.** Entries 10, 13, 14, 15, 17, 18, 19, 21, 26 and 31 are Grade
   9–10. Do not take entry 26 (mental-health prediction) into a Grade 7 room.
6. **Re-check links before a term starts.** Every link here was opened on 21 November
   2026. News sites move URLs; government PDFs get replaced. Each entry carries enough
   detail — title, author, date, publisher — to re-find the source if the link dies.
