Introduction

Consider a set of cards with the alphabet on top & you want to sort the shuffled cards. One way to go is to find the card A, then B and so on. The cards you have are punched on the top, and there is an interesting way to sort them. Insert a stick in the set of cards starting from the rightmost. Once you lift the stick, some will come along with it. Now, bring them in front & repeat this for all sets of holes. Magically, the cards are sorted !! Watch the video to discover the patterns in punching & the principle behind the trick.
The Punched Card Trick


Punched cards have a long history in computing, from the 19th century in data processing, machine control, and early computing, to the 20th century in early digital computers. The card used here has two types of holes in the top: a circular hole (denoted as 0) and a U slit (denoted as 1). It's a binary way of punching. The module starts with number representation in binary, its root in the Indian knowledge system (binary in the context of Sanskrit grammar & binary arithmetic by Acharya Pingala in Chandaḥśāstra 2 BCE) [1], sorting algorithm, various base representations in the punched card trick, and logical reasoning with cards.

Sorting Cards
Pick the deck of cards in your palm and shuffle them until they are well mixed and the order is disturbed. Now, follow these steps to get these cards in their right order:
1. Insert a stick into the rightmost hole or slit (whichever is on the right) and pull the cards up.
2. Some cards (say Type I) will get attached to the stick, and the rest of the cards tend to fall down (say Type II), which you hold up in your palm.
3. Place these cards (Type I) ahead (in front) of the remaining cards (Type II).
4. Now, place the stick in the second-rightmost hole; again, there will be some Type I cards and some Type II cards. Repeat step iii.
5. Repeat the process until the stick makes way to the leftmost hole/slit and club the Type I cards and place these cards ahead of the remaining cards. The cards will be arranged in order.

Logical Reasoning using Punched Cards
________________


In this activity, you will use punched cards to solve logical puzzles. Each hole on a card stands for a simple true/false (ON/OFF) condition, and each card represents one possible combination, like a row in a truth table. By stacking, aligning, and filtering the cards, we will see how complex reasoning can be built from simple binary decisions, mirroring how binary logic works inside a computer.


Binary System
On & Off
The five bulbs, Yellow, Pink, Red, Green, and Blue, are represented by the five holes, which in turn represent binary digits. Each 1 (or U slit hole) corresponds to a true term, or ‘ON’ state; each 0 (or O hole), to a false term, or ‘OFF’ state. Each card is a unique combination of true and false terms, and since the 32 cards exhaust all possible combinations, they are the equivalent of what is called a "truth table'' for the five terms. 
  
  
  

Figure: All possible cards with 5 binary digits

   2. Green bulb
   3. Red Bulb
   4. All bulbs are OFF
	

The operation of the cards is explained by showing how they can be used for solving a problem in two-valued logic. We can start by taking case 1, which contains 8 cards and focusing only on Red, Green, and Blue light. Three statements are given containing simple assertions and negation statements. The aim is to determine which bulbs are on or off among these. 


To solve this problem, start with the punch cards in any order. Only three terms are involved, so we shall be concerned with only the R, G, and B holes. 


R = Red bulb is ‘ON’ 
R̅ = Red bulb is ‘OFF’
G = Green bulb is ‘ON’
G̅ = Green bulb is ‘OFF’
B = Blue bulb is ‘ON’
B̅ = Blue bulb is ‘OFF’

Case : 1 RGB (with 8 cards only)


Statements 
A. The Red light is ‘ON’ 
B. The Blue light is ‘OFF’ 
C. ‘ON’ light only has ‘OFF’ Neighbour 


The problem has three statements. The first statement, A, "The red light is ‘ON’,” tells us that the combination R̅XX cards are not permitted, so we must eliminate all cards bearing this combination. It is done as follows. Insert the stick in the R position and lift. The stick will raise all cards bearing R̅ cards, the invalid combination, so these cards are discarded. All remaining cards are assembled into a pack once more (in any order), and we are ready for the second statement.

Similarly to the statement A, insert the stick in the B position and lift. The stick will raise all cards bearing B̅ cards, the valid combination, so these cards we need to keep; the cards in other hands need to be discarded. All remaining cards are assembled into a pack once more (in any order), and we are ready for the third statement.

The third statement, C, "‘ON’ light only has ‘OFF’ Neighbour,” here we need to recall the first statement that “The red light is ‘ON’,”  tells us that the Green bulb neighbouring the Red bulb should be ‘OFF’, that is, we need to eliminate the X1X combinations. Insert the stick in the G position and lift. The stick will raise all cards bearing G̅ cards, the invalid combination, so these cards are discarded. 

After this action, only one card will be left, and that will be the solution to the logical puzzle.

Ternary System
Gold, Silver & Rock
So far, we have used the binary approach (two hole types: O slit hole and U hole, representing 0 and 1, respectively) for logical reasoning. This time, we would like to explore logical reasoning using the ternary (also called base-3) number system.

The three treasures, A, B and C, are represented by the three positions. Each 0 (or O slit hole) corresponds to the presence of Gold in the treasure; each 1 (or V hole) corresponds to the presence of Silver in the treasure; and each 2 (or U hole) corresponds to the presence of Rock in the treasure, which represents the ternary system. 
  

Figure: Representation for Ternary system


Each card is a unique combination of these, and since the 27 cards exhaust all possible combinations, they are equivalent to a "truth table" for the three terms in ternary. 


  
  
  

Figure: All possible cards with 3 ternary digits 


In the ternary approach, we would use two different sticks: Needle stick which can be inserted in all three (O, V, U) holes but can escape (vertically) only through two (V, U) holes; and Wooden stick which can be inserted in all three (O, V, U) holes but can escape (vertically) only through one (U) hole.

Conclusion 
Punched cards, utilising binary choices represented by ON/OFF holes, enable the solution of logical puzzles in a manner analogous to their role in powering early computing machines such as IBM sorters and Turing's Bombe. Originating from Acharya Pingala's ancient binary concepts in the Chhandasutra (~200 BCE), this unplugged methodology promotes the development of logical reasoning and intuition.
